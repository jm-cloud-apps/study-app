# ti, ni, iti: The Marker Words

> **The one-sentence version:** Ilocano tags each noun with a small marker: **ti** (plural **dagiti**) for things in the core of the sentence, **iti** (plural **kadagiti**) for places, recipients and "some", and **ni / ken ni** for people's names.

## Why it matters

These markers are the glue of every sentence. They're how you hear who did what, what belongs to whom, and where something happened. The good news: Ilocano has **fewer of them than Tagalog**, so if Tagalog's *ang / ng / sa* ever felt crowded, this is a relief.

## The table

| | Core (subject, doer, owner) | Oblique (to / at / from / some) |
|---|---|---|
| **Things, one** | **ti** | **iti** |
| **Things, many** | **dagiti** | **kadagiti** |
| **A person's name** | **ni** | **ken ni** |
| **Names, several** | **da** | **kada** |

That's it: one core marker and one oblique marker, each with a plural and a version for names.

🔑 **The big difference from Tagalog: ti does the jobs of both *ang* and *ng*.** The subject, the doer and the owner all take **ti**. Ilocano tells them apart by **word order and the verb**, not by different markers.

## ti: the core marker

**The subject:**

> **Naimas ti pagnan.** — "The food is delicious."
> **Dakkel ti balay.** — "The house is big."

**The owner** ("of"):

> **ti balay ti maestra** — "the teacher's house" (*the house of the teacher*)
> **ti nagan ti aso** — "the dog's name"

**The doer** of a transitive verb (lesson 8). The doer usually comes right after the verb:

> **Tinarimaan ti mekaniko ti kotse.** — "The mechanic repaired the car."

The first **ti** noun after the verb is the mechanic (the doer), the second is the car.

## dagiti: more than one

**dagiti** is simply the plural of **ti**:

> **Naimas dagiti mangga.** — "The mangoes are delicious."
> **dagiti annak ti kabsatko** — "my sibling's children"

Ilocano can also show plural inside the noun by **doubling a sound**: **ubing** (child) → **ubbing** (children), **lalaki** (man) → **lallaki** (men), **anak** → **annak**. You'll often hear both: **dagiti ubbing**.

## iti: place, direction, recipient, and "some"

**iti** covers the "to / at / in / from" family:

> **Adda ti kuarta iti lamisaan.** — "The money is on the table."
> **Napanak iti eskuelaan.** — "I went to the school."

And it marks an **indefinite object** after an actor-focus verb (lesson 7): "a banana", "some bread".

> **Nangan iti saba.** — "He ate a banana."
> **Gimmatangak iti tinapay.** — "I bought (some) bread."

Compare: **ti saba** = *the* banana; **iti saba** = *a* banana. The marker carries the "the" vs "a" distinction that English puts in articles.

> 💡 **idiay** often replaces **iti** for a place that's far away or a named place: **Napanak idiay Vigan** ("I went to Vigan"). It's the "over there" word from lesson 5, used as "to/in".

## ni and ken ni: names

People's names take **ni** in the core:

> **Napintas ni Ana.** — "Ana is beautiful."
> **ti balay ni Ana** — "Ana's house"
> **Immay ni Lola.** — "Grandma came."

…and **ken ni** in the oblique:

> **Itedmo ken ni Ben.** — "Give it to Ben."
> **Nagsaoak ken ni Manang.** — "I talked to Manang."

For a group of named people, the plural versions are **da** (core) and **kada** (oblique).

> 💡 **ken** on its own means **and**: **ni Ana ken ni Ben**, **kape ken tinapay** ("coffee and bread").

## Kinship words can go either way

Words like **nanang** (mom), **tatang** (dad), **lola** and **maestra** (teacher) can take **ni** (treated like a name: *my* mom, a specific person) or **ti** (treated as a common noun: *a* mother, any teacher):

> **Napan ti ama idiay eskuelaan ti anakna.** — "The father went to his child's school." (general: *a* father)
> **Napan ni Tatang idiay eskuelaan ni kabsat.** — "Dad went to my sibling's school." (specific: *my* dad)

Using **ni** with a kin word sounds personal and specific; you'll hear it all the time inside a family.

## "And", "but", "or": the joining words

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **ken** | and (between nouns) | **kape ken gatas** — coffee and milk |
| **ket** | and (between clauses); also links a topic | **Napanak idiay, ket nangan.** — I went there and ate. |
| **ngem** | but | **Nalaing, ngem nabannog.** — Skilled, but tired. |
| **wenno** | or | **kape wenno tsaa** — coffee or tea |

## Key takeaways

- **ti / dagiti** = core (subject, owner, doer); **iti / kadagiti** = oblique (place, recipient, "some").
- **ni / da** for names in the core; **ken ni / kada** in the oblique.
- **ti** covers Tagalog's *ang* **and** *ng*; word order (doer right after the verb) does the sorting.
- **iti** + object after an actor verb = *a/some* thing; **ti** = *the* thing.
- **idiay** for far or named places: **idiay Vigan**.
- **ken** = and (nouns), **ket** = and (clauses), **ngem** = but, **wenno** = or.
