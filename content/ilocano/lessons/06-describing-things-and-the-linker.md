# Describing Things, and the Linker a / nga

> **The one-sentence version:** Most Ilocano adjectives start with **na-** (*napintas*, *naimas*, *nalaing*); you join an adjective to its noun with the linker **a** or **nga**; and **unay**, **nag-…-en** and **ka-…-an** turn "good" into "very good", "so good!" and "the best".

## Why it matters

Describing things is how you compliment food, react to weather, and talk about people, which are three of the most common things Ilocanos say to each other. The linker is also the glue you'll use in many other places (demonstratives, negation, "want to"), so learning it here pays off everywhere.

## Adjectives with na-

Most describing words are **na-** + a root:

| Adjective | Meaning | Root |
|---|---|---|
| **napintas** | beautiful, nice | *pintas* (beauty) |
| **naimbag** | good, kind | *imbag* |
| **naimas** | delicious | *imas* |
| **nalaing** | clever, skilled | *laing* |
| **napudot** | hot | *pudot* (heat) |
| **nalam-ek** | cold | *lam-ek* |
| **nangina** | expensive | *ngina* |
| **nalaka** | cheap, easy | *laka* |
| **nasayaat** | fine, nice, well | *sayaat* |
| **nabannog** | tired | *bannog* |
| **nadalus** | clean | *dalus* |

A few common ones don't use **na-**: **dakkel** (big), **bassit** (small), **baro** (new), **adu** (many, a lot).

Like everything else, an adjective can be a whole sentence on its own, with the subject after it:

> **Napintas ti balaymo.** — "Your house is beautiful."
> **Napudot ita.** — "It's hot today."
> **Nabannogak.** — "I'm tired."

## The linker: a or nga

To join an adjective to a noun, Ilocano puts a small **linker** between them, either **a** or **nga**:

> **napintas a balay** — "a beautiful house"
> **naimbag nga aldaw** — "a good day"
> **dakkel a problema** — "a big problem"
> **bassit nga ubing** — "a small child"

🔑 **A good working rule: nga before a word that starts with a vowel, a before a consonant.** That's why it's **naimbag nga aldaw** (*aldaw* starts with a vowel) but **naimbag a bigat** (*bigat* starts with a consonant). Some speakers use **nga** more widely, so you'll hear exceptions; nobody will misunderstand you either way.

The order can flip, with the same linker in the middle:

> **napintas a balay** = **balay a napintas** — "a beautiful house"

Adjective-first is more common in speech.

> 💡 The linker isn't only for adjectives. You've already met it with demonstratives (**daytoy a balay**), and you'll meet it with **saan** ("not", lesson 9) and after **kayat** ("want", lesson 9). Whenever two pieces of a phrase need joining, **a / nga** is the glue.

## Very, so, and the most

| Pattern | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| adjective + **unay** | **naimas unay** | very delicious |
| **nag-** + root + **-en** | **nagimasen!** | so delicious! |
| **nag-** + root + **-en** | **nagpudoten!** | it's so hot! |
| **ka-** + root + **-an** | **ti kaimasan** | the most delicious |
| **ka-** + root + **-an** | **ti kapudotan** | the hottest |

**unay** is the everyday "very". **nag-…-en** is an exclamation, the thing you say with feeling when the food is amazing or the heat is unbearable. **ka-…-an** picks out the top one:

> **Daytoy ti kaimasan a pinakbet!** — "This is the most delicious pinakbet!"

## Comparing: ngem

**ngem** ("but") also means "**than**". Put **ad-adda** ("more") before the adjective:

> **Ad-adda a napudot ditoy ngem idiay Baguio.** — "It's hotter here than in Baguio."

In casual speech people often skip **ad-adda** and let **ngem** do the work, or simply contrast: **Napudot ditoy, ngem nalam-ek idiay Baguio.** ("It's hot here, but cold in Baguio.")

## Compliments you'll actually use

| Ilocano | Meaning | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Naimas!** | Delicious! | At every meal you're served |
| **Nagimasen!** | So delicious! | When you really mean it |
| **Napintas!** | Beautiful! / Nice! | Views, clothes, babies, photos |
| **Nalaingka!** | You're so good / clever! | Encouragement |
| **Nasayaat!** | Great, nice | Plans, news |

> ⚠️ Complimenting food is close to obligatory at an Ilocano table, and saying **Naimas!** to the cook is the quickest way to win a grandmother over. Saying it more than once is not overdoing it.

## Check yourself

3. Say "a small house" two ways, with the adjective first and then the noun first.

## Key takeaways

- Most adjectives are **na-** + root: *napintas, naimas, nalaing, napudot*. Some aren't: **dakkel, bassit, baro, adu**.
- An adjective can be the whole predicate: **Nabannogak.** = I'm tired.
- Join adjective and noun with **a / nga**: roughly **nga before a vowel**, **a before a consonant**.
- **unay** = very; **nag-…-en** = "so …!"; **ka-…-an** = the most.
- **ngem** = but / than; **ad-adda** = more.
- Compliment the food: **Naimas!**
