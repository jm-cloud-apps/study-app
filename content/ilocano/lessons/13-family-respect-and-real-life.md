# Family, Respect, and Real-Life Ilocano

> **The one-sentence version:** Respect in Ilocano runs through **apo** and the plural **kayo**; family words like **manong**, **manang** and **ading** are used with everyone; food is how people show love; and if you know Tagalog, a few false friends (**saan**, **bigat**, **sabong**) are waiting for you.

## Why it matters

Grammar gets your sentences right; this lesson gets the *relationship* right. Most learners come to Ilocano through family, often grandparents, and the words in this lesson are the ones that make older speakers feel respected, and make you feel like part of the family rather than a visitor.

## apo: the word of respect

**apo** is the Ilocano marker of respect, used where Tagalog uses *po*. It goes at the end of a phrase when you speak to an elder, a stranger or anyone you want to honour:

> **Wen, apo.** — "Yes (respectfully)."
> **Agyamanak, apo.** — "Thank you (respectfully)."
> **Naimbag a bigat, apo.** — "Good morning (respectfully)."

**apo** has other meanings too: it means **grandchild**, and **Apo** is a title of honour (**Apo Dios**, "Lord God"). Context makes it clear.

🔑 **Respect also comes from kayo.** Addressing one older person, switch from **-ka / sika** to the plural **-kayo / dakayo**: **Kumustakayo, apo?** This is the T-V distinction you know from Spanish *tú / usted*, and it matters more than any single word.

## Family words

| Ilocano | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **nanang** / **inang** | mother |
| **tatang** / **amang** | father |
| **lola** / **apong baket** | grandmother |
| **lolo** / **apong lakay** | grandfather |
| **kabsat** | sibling |
| **manong** | older brother; any older man |
| **manang** | older sister; any older woman |
| **ading** | younger sibling; anyone younger |
| **uliteg** | uncle |
| **ikit** | aunt |
| **kasinsin** | cousin |
| **asawa** | spouse |
| **anak** / **annak** | child / children (offspring) |
| **apo** | grandchild |

> 💡 **Manong** and **manang** are Ilocano words that spread across the Philippines. In Hawaii and California, "Manong" became an affectionate name for the first generation of Ilocano labourers. Using them for strangers is warm, not presumptuous: **Manong, sadino ti terminal?**

**lakay** and **baket** on their own mean "old man" and "old woman", and also, affectionately, "husband" and "wife": **ni lakayko** ("my husband").

## Visiting an Ilocano home

| Moment | What to say |
|---|---|
| Arriving | **Naimbag a malem, apo!** · **Addadtoyak!** (I'm here!) |
| Being offered food | **Agyamanak, apo.** (then eat!) |
| Eating | **Naimas!** · **Nagimasen!** |
| Being offered more | **Bassit laeng, apo.** (just a little) |
| Leaving | **Innakon, apo. Agyamanak unay!** |

> **Mangantayon!** — "Let's eat now!" You'll hear this the moment food is ready, often as the first thing said to anyone who walks in.

Ilocano food is famous and worth knowing by name: **pinakbet** (vegetables stewed with bagoong), **dinengdeng** (a vegetable broth), **bagnet** (crispy pork), **Vigan longganisa**, **empanada** from Batac and Vigan, and **basi** (sugarcane wine). Naming a dish you love is an instant conversation.

## A little culture

- **Vigan**, in Ilocos Sur, is a UNESCO World Heritage city of Spanish-era streets, and the **burnay** clay jars made there gave this topic its emoji.
- **Biag ni Lam-ang** ("The Life of Lam-ang") is the Ilocano epic, about a hero who can talk at birth and is brought back to life by his rooster and dog.
- **Pamulinawen** and **Manang Biday** are folk songs nearly every Ilocano family knows. Learn a verse and you'll be asked to sing it.
- The Ilocano weekly magazine **Bannawag** has published stories and poetry for decades.

## If you know Tagalog: the false friends

| Word | In Tagalog | In Ilocano |
|---|---|---|
| **saan** | where | **no** |
| **bigat** | heavy | **morning** |
| **sabong** | cockfight | **flower** |
| **mano** | the hand-blessing gesture | **how much / how many** |
| **kita** | we/I-you; income | **to see** (also "kind, type") |

And the near-misses that are actually helpful: **ditoy** (*dito*), **aldaw** (*araw*), **adda** (like *may/mayroon*), **laeng** (*lang*), **gayam** (*pala*), **kano** (*daw*).

> ⚠️ Answering **Saan** to "Do you want some?" means **no**. More than one Tagalog-speaking guest has gone hungry this way.

## Mixing with English and Tagalog

Real Ilocano, like real Tagalog, borrows freely. English verbs take Ilocano prefixes and endings: **Nag-text-ak kenka** ("I texted you"), **Ag-drive-ak** ("I'll drive"). Many Ilocanos also speak Tagalog and English, so switching mid-conversation is normal, especially among younger speakers and in the diaspora. Don't be embarrassed to fill a gap with an English word; just keep the Ilocano frame (the markers, the pronoun endings) around it.

## Check yourself

1. Greet your grandmother in the morning respectfully, using both **apo** and **kayo**.
2. A Tagalog-speaking friend says **"Mabigat"** and an Ilocano speaker looks confused. What might the Ilocano speaker be thinking of?
3. You're full and your host offers more. What do you say?

## Key takeaways

- **apo** = respect (like *po*); also grandchild and a title of honour.
- Use **-kayo / dakayo** for one respected person: **Kumustakayo, apo?**
- **manong / manang / ading** for older men / older women / younger people, family or not.
- Food is love: **Mangantayon!**, **Naimas!**, **Bassit laeng, apo.**
- False friends with Tagalog: **saan** (no), **bigat** (morning), **sabong** (flower), **mano** (how much).
- Borrowing English is normal: keep the Ilocano frame around it.
