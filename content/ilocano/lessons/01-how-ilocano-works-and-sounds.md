# How Ilocano Works (and How to Pronounce It)

> **The one-sentence version:** Ilocano is the big language of northern Luzon. Like Tagalog, it puts the predicate first and uses little marker words instead of word order, and its sound you'll notice first is the **doubled consonant**: *uppat*, *dakkel*, *bassit*.

Welcome. Whether you're learning Ilocano to talk with a grandparent, to visit the Ilocos, or because it's the language you half-heard growing up, you're in the right place. This lesson gives you the map: where the language lives, how its sentences are shaped, and how to say what you read.

## Where Ilocano is spoken

**Ilocano** (also spelled **Ilokano**, and called **Iloko** by many speakers) is the **third most spoken native language of the Philippines**, with roughly 8–9 million first-language speakers and around 2 million more who speak it as a second language.

- Its home is **northern Luzon**: Ilocos Norte, Ilocos Sur, La Union (where it's an official provincial language), Abra, and much of the **Cagayan Valley** (Cagayan, Isabela, Nueva Vizcaya).
- It's the **lingua franca of the Cordillera**: many Igorot communities with their own languages use Ilocano to talk to each other.
- Ilocanos migrated widely, to Central Luzon, Mindoro, Palawan and Mindanao, and abroad. In **Hawaii**, the large majority of Filipinos are Ilocano speakers, and there are big communities in California and Canada.

Speakers have an old name for their language: **Samtoy**, from *sao mi ditoy*, "our language here."

> 💡 Ilocano and Tagalog are related, but they are **different languages**, not dialects of each other. A Tagalog speaker won't follow an Ilocano conversation. If you already know Tagalog, though, you'll find the *machinery* familiar, and lesson 13 lists the traps.

## The shape of a sentence

**1. The predicate comes first.** The news leads; the thing you're talking about comes after:

> **Napintas ni Ana.** — *beautiful / Ana* = "Ana is beautiful."
> **Naimas ti taraon.** — *delicious / the food* = "The food is delicious."

There's no verb "to be". The two halves just sit side by side.

**2. Markers tag each noun's job.** **ti**, **iti** and **ni** in front of nouns tell you who is who (lesson 4).

**3. Pronouns are tiny endings that stick to the first word.** "I'm hungry" is one word: **Mabisinak** (*mabisin* + *-ak*). Lesson 3 is about these endings, and they're the real key to sounding Ilocano.

**4. Verbs change shape for focus and aspect**, much as in Tagalog (lessons 7 and 8).

## The vowels

| Letter | Sounds like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **a** | *a* in "father" | **adda** (there is) |
| **e** | see below | **ket** (and), **dengngen** (listen) |
| **i** | *ee* in "see" (short) | **ita** (now) |
| **o** | *o* in "or" | **dakayo** (you all) |
| **u** | *oo* in "food" | **uppat** (four) |

🔑 **The letter e is the one to watch.** In native Ilocano words, many speakers (especially in the Cagayan Valley and southern areas) pronounce **e** as a vowel English doesn't have, made with the tongue in the middle of the mouth, a little like the *u* in "put" said without rounding the lips. In the Ilocos provinces themselves you'll often hear a plain *e* as in "bet". In Spanish loanwords (**elepante**, **estudiante**) it's always the plain *e*. Imitate the people you're actually talking to.

> 💡 As in Tagalog, **o** and **u** blur in some words. You'll see both *sangapulo* and *sangapuló* for "ten", and some spellings use **o** where others use **u**.

## Doubled consonants are really doubled

This is Ilocano's signature sound. When a consonant is written twice, you **hold it**, the way English holds the *k* in "boo**k-c**ase":

| Word | Meaning | Say it |
|---|---|---|
| **uppat** | four | *oop-pat* |
| **innem** | six | *in-nem* |
| **tallo** | three | *tal-lo* |
| **dakkel** | big | *dak-kel* |
| **bassit** | small | *bas-sit* |
| **adda** | there is / has | *ad-da* |

Don't skip the double. **Annak** (children, offspring) and **anak** (a child) are different words.

## The glottal stop and the hyphen

The **glottal stop** is the catch in "uh-oh." Ilocano writes it, when it falls in the middle of a word, with a **hyphen**:

> **nalam-ek** (cold) · **ay-ayaten** (to love) · **sam-it** (sweetness)

Between two identical vowels it's there even without a hyphen: **saan** (no) is *sa-an*, with a catch in the middle.

## The consonants

- **ng** is one sound, as in "si**ng**", and it can start a word: **ngem** (but), **ngarud** (so then).
- **r** is tapped, like Spanish: **rabii** (night).
- **Kur-itan** was the name of the old indigenous script, a relative of Tagalog baybayin. Today Ilocano is written in the Latin alphabet.

## Stress can change meaning

Most words stress the second-to-last or the last syllable, and it isn't usually written. Occasionally it's the only difference between two words:

| Stress | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **KA**-yo | wood, tree |
| ka-**YO** | you (plural or polite) |
| **KI**-ta | kind, type |
| ki-**TA** | to see |

Context almost always makes it obvious. Don't memorise stress from a page; copy it from voices.

## Two spellings you'll see

Older Ilocano writing used **Spanish-style spelling** (*c*, *qu*, *gu*: *quita*, *cayo*). Modern writing, including the long-running Ilocano magazine **Bannawag**, uses spelling closer to Tagalog's (*kita*, *kayo*). This course uses the modern spelling, but you'll meet the older one in church books and on old signs.

## Key takeaways

- Ilocano is the **main language of northern Luzon** and the lingua franca of the Cordillera, with a large diaspora (especially in **Hawaii**).
- It's related to Tagalog but is a **separate language**.
- **Predicate first, no verb "to be"**, markers for noun roles, and **pronoun endings** attached to the first word.
- **Hold doubled consonants**: *uppat*, *dakkel*, *bassit*.
- A **hyphen** marks a glottal stop: *nalam-ek*.
- The vowel **e** varies by region; copy the speakers around you.
