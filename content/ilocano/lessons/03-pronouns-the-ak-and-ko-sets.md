# Pronouns: the -ak Set and the -ko Set

> **The one-sentence version:** Ilocano pronouns are mostly **endings glued onto the first word of the sentence**. There are two sets: the **-ak set** ("I" as the one the sentence is about) and the **-ko set** ("by me" and "my"), plus stand-alone forms for emphasis.

## Why it matters

Pronouns are in nearly every sentence, and in Ilocano they don't sit where English puts them. "I'm hungry", "my house" and "I saw it" all hide their pronoun at the end of a word. Once you can spot these endings, half of what you hear stops sounding like one long word.

🔑 **If you know Tagalog, this is the ako/ko split, shrunk into suffixes.** The **-ak** set does the job of *ako, ka, siya…*; the **-ko** set does the job of *ko, mo, niya…*.

## The -ak set: who the sentence is about

| Ending | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **-ak** | I | **Mabisinak.** — I'm hungry. |
| **-ka** | you (one) | **Nalaingka.** — You're clever. |
| *(nothing)* | he / she / it | **Nabannog.** — He/she's tired. |
| **-ta** | we two (you and I) | **Mapanta!** — Let's go (the two of us)! |
| **-tayo** | we all (including you) | **Mangantayo!** — Let's eat! |
| **-kami** | we (not you) | **Taga-Ilocos kami.** — We're from Ilocos. |
| **-kayo** | you (several, or polite) | **Kumustakayo?** |
| **-da** | they | **Immayda.** — They came. |

Two things English speakers don't expect:

- **"He/she" has no ending at all.** *Nabannog* alone means "he/she is tired." Context tells you who.
- **There are three kinds of "we".** **-ta** is you and me only; **-tayo** is all of us, including you; **-kami** is us but *not* you. Inviting a friend to eat: **Mangantayo!** Telling a host who offers food that you've already eaten: **Nangankamin** ("we've eaten already"), using *kami* because the host wasn't eating with you.

## The -ko set: doer and owner

| Ending | Meaning | As owner | As doer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **-ko / -k** | my / by me | **balayko** (my house) | **Ammok.** — I know (it). |
| **-mo / -m** | your / by you | **naganmo** (your name) | **Kayatmo?** — Do you want (it)? |
| **-na** | his / her | **asawana** (her husband) | **Kinitana.** — She looked at it. |
| **-ta** | our (you and me) | **lugarta** | |
| **-tayo** | our (all of us) | **pagiliantayo** (our country) | |
| **-mi** | our (not you) | **balaymi** | |
| **-yo** | your (several / polite) | **naganyo** | |
| **-da** | their | **annakda** (their children) | |

🔑 **-ko and -mo shrink to -k and -m after a vowel**: *asawa* + *ko* = **asawak** (my spouse), *aso* + *mo* = **asom** (your dog), *ammo* + *ko* = **ammok** (I know). After a consonant they stay whole: **naganko**, **balaymo**.

> 💡 **-ta**, **-tayo** and **-da** look the same in both sets. That's fine: their position and the verb tell you which job they're doing.

## Stand-alone pronouns

When a pronoun is the whole answer, is emphasised, or comes first:

| Stand-alone | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **siak** | I, me |
| **sika** | you |
| **isu** / **isuna** | he, she |
| **data** | we two |
| **datayo** | we all (incl. you) |
| **dakami** | we (excl. you) |
| **dakayo** | you all / you (polite) |
| **isuda** | they |

> **Siasino ti napan?** — "Who went?" — **Siak.** — "Me."
> **Sika ti nalaing.** — "*You're* the clever one."

## "To me, with you": the oblique forms

After a word that needs "to / for / with", use these:

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **kaniak** | to/for me |
| **kenka** | to/for you |
| **kenkuana** | to/for him/her |
| **kadakami** · **kadatayo** · **kadakayo** · **kadakuada** | to us / us all / you all / them |

> **Itedmo kaniak.** — "Give it to me."
> **Adda ti tulbek kaniak.** — "I have the key (on me)."

## Where the endings go

🔑 **The ending attaches to the first word of the sentence**, whatever that word is. Watch the **-ak** move:

> **Napanak idiay Vigan.** — "I went to Vigan."
> **Saanak a napan idiay Vigan.** — "I didn't go to Vigan."

In the second sentence **saan** ("not") came first, so **-ak** jumped onto it. This is exactly the habit that Tagalog's *ako* has, made more visible.

With **di** (another "not"), the -ko ending becomes **diak**: **Diak ammo** ("I don't know"), not *diko*.

## "You" and "me" together

When I do something to you, Ilocano fuses the two pronouns into one ending:

> **Ay-ayatenka.** — "I love you." (*ayaten* + *ka*: by me, you)
> **Tulongannak.** — "Help me." (by you, me)

You'll meet more of these combinations as you listen. Start with these two: you'll use both.

## Check yourself

1. Say "I'm tired" (*nabannog*) and "They're tired."
2. Why does "my spouse" come out as **asawak** but "my house" as **balayko**?
3. You and a friend are leaving a party together. Which "we" do you use in **Mapan___!**, and why?

## Key takeaways

- **-ak set** (*-ak, -ka, ∅, -ta, -tayo, -kami, -kayo, -da*): the one the sentence is about.
- **-ko set** (*-ko/-k, -mo/-m, -na, -ta, -tayo, -mi, -yo, -da*): owner or doer.
- "He/she" in the -ak set is **nothing at all**.
- Three "we"s: **-ta** (you and me), **-tayo** (all of us), **-kami** (us, not you).
- Stand-alone: **siak, sika, isuna, dakami, datayo, dakayo, isuda**. Oblique: **kaniak, kenka, kenkuana…**
- Endings stick to the **first word**, including *saan* and *di* (**diak** = I don't).
