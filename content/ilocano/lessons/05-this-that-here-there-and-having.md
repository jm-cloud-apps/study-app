# This, That, Here, There, and Having

> **The one-sentence version:** Ilocano points with three distances (**daytoy** near me, **dayta** near you, **daydiay** far from us both), says "there is" and "have" with one word, **adda**, and "there isn't" and "don't have" with **awan**.

## Why it matters

Pointing and having are what you do most as a beginner: *this one*, *how much is that?*, *is there coffee?*, *I don't have any.* With the words in this lesson you can shop, order, and ask where things are before you know a single verb.

## Three distances, not two

English has *this* and *that*. Ilocano has a three-way split, and the middle one is anchored to **the listener**:

| | Near me | Near you | Far from both |
|---|---|---|---|
| **this / that** | **daytoy** | **dayta** | **daydiay** |
| **these / those** | **dagitoy** | **dagita** | **dagidiay** |
| **here / there** | **ditoy** | **dita** | **idiay** |

> **Ania daytoy?** — "What's this?" (in my hand)
> **Ania dayta?** — "What's that?" (the thing you're holding)
> **Ania daydiay?** — "What's that over there?"

🔑 **dayta means "that, by you".** If someone's holding a phone and you ask about it, it's **dayta**, even if it's only a metre away. Choosing by the listener's position is the instinct to build.

> 💡 In fast speech the demonstratives shorten: **'toy**, **'ta**, **'diay**. **'Ta lalaki** = "that man." You'll hear these constantly and rarely see them written.

## Using them with a noun

Put the demonstrative before the noun and join them with the linker **a / nga** (lesson 6):

> **daytoy a balay** — "this house"
> **dayta nga aso** — "that dog (near you)"
> **dagidiay nga ubbing** — "those children over there"

Or use them alone, as the subject:

> **Naimas daytoy.** — "This is delicious."
> **Kukuak dayta.** — "That's mine."

## Here and there

> **Umayka ditoy.** — "Come here."
> **Adda dita ti libro?** — "Is the book there (by you)?"
> **Napanak idiay Laoag.** — "I went to Laoag."

**idiay** does double duty: "over there" and, as you saw in lesson 4, "to / in" before a named or distant place. **Idiay Hawaii**, **idiay Manila**, **idiay balay** (at home).

## adda: there is, and have

**adda** says something exists, or that someone has it:

> **Adda kape?** — "Is there coffee?"
> **Adda kadi kotsem?** — "Do you have a car?" (*kotse* + *-m*)
> **Adda asawak.** — "I have a spouse."

Notice **adda** + noun + owner ending: *kotse-m*, *asawa-k*. You don't need a verb "to have"; you say "there exists my spouse."

For "I have it *on me* right now", put the owner in the oblique:

> **Adda ti tulbek kaniak.** — "I have the key (with me)."

**adda** joins with *here/there* into handy one-word forms:

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **addadtoy** | is here |
| **addadta** | is there (by you) |
| **addadiay** | is over there |

> **Addadtoyak!** — "I'm here!" (the classic thing to call out when you arrive)

## awan: there isn't, and don't have

**awan** is the opposite of **adda**:

> **Awan ti kape.** — "There's no coffee."
> **Awan ti luganko.** — "I don't have a car." (*lugan* = vehicle)
> **Awan ti aso ni Maria.** — "Maria doesn't have a dog."
> **Awan kaniak.** — "I don't have it (on me)."

Notice that **awan** is followed by **ti**: "absent is the coffee."

🔑 **Use awan, not saan, to deny existence.** "There's no rice" is **Awan ti bagas**, never *saan a bagas*. **saan** negates verbs, adjectives and identities (lesson 9).

**adda** and **awan** also make "someone" and "no one":

> **Adda ti immay.** — "Someone came."
> **Awan ti immay.** — "No one came."

## Where is it? ayan

To ask where something is, use **ayan** ("location") with an owner ending:

> **Ayanna ti banio?** — "Where's the bathroom?" (*literally* "its location, the bathroom")
> **Ayanna ni Lola?** — "Where's Grandma?"
> **Ayanmo?** — "Where are you?" (on the phone)

And answer with the words above: **Addadiay.** ("Over there.") **Addadtoy.** ("Here.")

## Mine and yours

**kukua** ("property") + an owner ending makes *mine, yours*:

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **kukuak** | mine |
| **kukuam** | yours |
| **kukuana** | his / hers |
| **kukuami** · **kukuatayo** · **kukuayo** · **kukuada** | ours (not you) · ours (all) · yours (pl.) · theirs |

> **Kukuam kadi daytoy?** — "Is this yours?" — **Wen, kukuak.** — "Yes, it's mine."

## Check yourself

1. Your friend across the table is holding a plate. Do you ask **Ania daytoy?** or **Ania dayta?**, and why?
2. Say "There's no water" (*danum*). Why is **saan** the wrong word here?
3. You arrive at a relative's house and want to announce yourself. What do you call out?

## Key takeaways

- **daytoy / dayta / daydiay**: near me / near you / far. Plurals **dagitoy / dagita / dagidiay**.
- **ditoy / dita / idiay**: here / there by you / over there (and "to/in" a named place).
- **adda** = there is / have: **Adda kadi kotsem?**; **adda … kaniak** = I have it on me.
- **awan** = there isn't / don't have: **Awan ti kape.** Use it instead of *saan* for existence.
- **Ayanna ti ___?** = Where's the ___? **Addadtoy / addadiay** = here / over there.
- **kukuak / kukuam / kukuana** = mine / yours / theirs.
