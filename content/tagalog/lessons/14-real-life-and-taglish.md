# Real Life: Getting Around, and the Taglish Everyone Actually Speaks

> **The one-sentence version:** In Metro Manila people speak Tagalog and English blended together in one sentence — and this isn't broken language, it's the actual, prestigious, everyday register, with English words slotted neatly into Tagalog grammar.

## Why it matters

If you study only textbook Tagalog and then land in Manila, you'll be surprised — not because people speak too fast, but because half the words are English. Understanding *how* the blending works turns that from a wall into a shortcut, because you already know half the vocabulary.

## How Taglish actually works

It isn't random. **The grammar stays Tagalog; the content words are often English.** Most strikingly, Tagalog verb affixes attach happily to English roots:

| Taglish | What's happening | English |
|---|---|---|
| **nag-shopping** | *nag-* (completed) + shopping | went shopping |
| **mag-meeting tayo** | *mag-* (contemplated) + meeting | let's have a meeting |
| **i-check mo** | *i-* (object focus) + check | check it |
| **na-late ako** | *na-* (completed, accidental) + late | I was late |
| **in-approve na** | *-in-* (completed) + approve | it's been approved |
| **pa-print** | *pa-* (request) + print | please print / get it printed |
| **nag-e-enjoy** | *nag-* + reduplicated *e* + enjoy | is enjoying |

Look at **nag-e-enjoy** — that's the reduplication rule from lesson 7 applied to an English word. The grammar is doing exactly what you learned; only the root has changed.

So everything you've studied still applies. You're not learning a third language.

| Fully Tagalog | Everyday Taglish |
|---|---|
| **Pupunta ako sa palengke bukas.** | **Pupunta ako sa market bukas.** |
| **Masyadong mahal ang bahay.** | **Sobrang expensive ng house.** |
| **Nagluluto ako ng hapunan.** | **Nagko-cook ako ng dinner.** |

> 💡 **Register, not laziness.** In Metro Manila, code-switching is normal in offices, media, and among friends; deep, unmixed Tagalog can read as formal, provincial, or literary depending on context. Neither is "better." As a learner, aim to *understand* Taglish and to *produce* whichever you're comfortable with — nobody will fault you for either.

## Eating out

| Tagalog | English |
|---|---|
| **Isa pong ___, please.** | One ___, please. |
| **Pahingi po ng tubig.** | Could I have some water? |
| **Ano po'ng masarap dito?** | What's good here? |
| **Para sa akin, ___.** | For me, ___. |
| **Take out po.** | To go. |
| **Dine in po.** | To eat here. |
| **Pa-bill po.** / **Bill out po.** | The bill, please. |
| **Masarap po!** | It's delicious! |
| **Busog na ako.** | I'm full. |

**Pahingi** ("please give me") and **pabili** ("please buy me / I'd like to buy") are two of the most useful words in daily life:

> **Pabili po ng load.** — "I'd like to buy phone credit." *(said at any sari-sari store)*

A **sari-sari store** is the tiny neighborhood shop selling everything in single-serving quantities — the center of gravity of any Filipino street.

## Getting around

| Tagalog | English |
|---|---|
| **Para po!** | Stop, please! *(to a jeepney driver)* |
| **Bayad po.** | Here's my fare. *(passing money forward)* |
| **Sa tabi lang po.** | Just at the side, please. *(where to drop you)* |
| **Saan po ang sakayan ng ___?** | Where do I catch the ___? |
| **Malayo pa ba?** | Is it still far? |
| **Ilang minuto?** | How many minutes? |
| **Traffic!** | *(a complete, universally understood sentence)* |

**Para po!** is from Spanish *parar*, to stop. On a jeepney you say it out loud, and you pass your fare forward through other passengers, who will hand it along without being asked. **Bayad po** is what you say as you do.

## Small talk that will happen to you

Filipinos are direct in a way that surprises some visitors — questions about your age, salary, marital status and weight are conversational warmth, not intrusion.

| Tagalog | English |
|---|---|
| **Kumain ka na?** | Have you eaten? *(the universal greeting)* |
| **Saan ka nakatira?** | Where do you live? |
| **Ano'ng trabaho mo?** | What's your work? |
| **May asawa ka na ba?** | Are you married? |
| **Ilang taon ka na?** | How old are you? |
| **Taga-saan ka?** | Where are you from? |
| **Bakit ka nandito sa Pilipinas?** | Why are you here in the Philippines? |

You're allowed to deflect with humor — **"Sikreto!"** ("secret!") — and nobody takes offense.

## Words with no English equivalent

Every language has these, and Tagalog's are especially good:

| Tagalog | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **kilig** | the giddy, fluttery thrill of romance — butterflies, as a feeling you can name |
| **gigil** | the trembling urge to squeeze something unbearably cute |
| **tampo** | withdrawing affection when hurt — sulking, but softer and more relational; you don't announce it, you just go quiet |
| **lambing** | affectionate sweetness, cuddliness; the cure for *tampo* |
| **sayang** | "what a waste" — regret for something wasted or a missed chance |
| **bahala na** | "come what may" — a leap into uncertainty, part fatalism, part courage |
| **pasalubong** | a gift you bring home for people from a trip |
| **tabi** | (asking the unseen to step aside — said when passing certain places at night) |

**Tampo** is worth understanding if you're in a Filipino family. It's not the silent treatment as punishment; it's a recognized emotional state that expects gentle coaxing (**lambing**) rather than confrontation. Knowing the word is half of navigating it.

**Bahala na** is sometimes translated as fatalism, but that undersells it — it's closer to "I've done what I can; I'll take the leap." It's said before hard things, not to avoid them.

## Texting and online

Filipino texting is heavily abbreviated, and you'll meet it immediately:

| Text | Full | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **q** / **ko** | ako / ko | I / my |
| **kmusta** | kumusta | how are you |
| **slmt** | salamat | thanks |
| **pnu** | paano | how |
| **d2** | dito | here |
| **wla** | wala | none |
| **ung** / **yung** | iyong | the / that |
| **nmn** | naman | (softener) |
| **po** | po | (still there — politeness survives abbreviation) |

Also universal: **"hahaha"** (any length), **"charot"** / **"char"** (just kidding), **"sana all"** (roughly "I wish that were me too"), and **"G"** (game — I'm in, let's do it).

## Where to go from here

You've covered the structural spine of the language: markers, pronouns, aspect, focus, particles, and the social layer. That's genuinely most of the grammar. What's left is mostly vocabulary and hours of listening.

The highest-return things now:

1. **Watch with Tagalog audio.** Teleseryes, Filipino YouTube, movies. Don't chase full comprehension — chase the rhythm, the particles, and the moments you catch a whole sentence.
2. **Listen to OPM.** Songs are memorized grammar you'll never lose. Ben&Ben, Eraserheads, APO Hiking Society, Moira, Up Dharma Down — a range of eras and speeds.
3. **Ask your one magic question** — **"Ano'ng ibig sabihin ng ___?"** — relentlessly.
4. **Speak badly, on purpose, early.** The single biggest predictor of progress. Filipinos are famously encouraging with learners, and the phrase you need is **"Nag-aaral akong mag-Tagalog"** — "I'm learning Tagalog." It reliably produces smiles and help.

**Kaya mo 'yan.** — You've got this.

## Check yourself

1. Break down **nag-e-enjoy** into its parts. Which lesson's rule is doing the work in the middle?
2. You're on a jeepney and your stop is coming. What two things do you say, and when?
3. What's the difference between **tampo** and simply being angry — and what's the usual remedy?

## Key takeaways

- **Taglish keeps Tagalog grammar and swaps in English words** — including verb affixes on English roots: *nag-shopping, i-check mo, na-late ako*.
- Code-switching is a normal, prestigious Metro Manila register, not broken language.
- Restaurant and store essentials: **Pahingi po**, **Pabili po**, **Pa-bill po**, **Take out po**.
- Transport: **Para po!** to stop a jeepney, **Bayad po** when passing your fare.
- Personal questions are warmth, not intrusion — and **"Sikreto!"** is a fine deflection.
- Untranslatable words worth owning: **kilig, gigil, tampo, lambing, sayang, bahala na, pasalubong**.
- Progress comes from listening and from speaking badly early. **"Nag-aaral akong mag-Tagalog"** opens every door.
