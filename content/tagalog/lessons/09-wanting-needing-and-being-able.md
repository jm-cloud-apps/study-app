# Verbs, Part 3: Wanting, Needing, Being Able, and Telling People Things

> **The one-sentence version:** A small set of words — **gusto, ayaw, kailangan, puwede, dapat, kaya** — let you express want, need, permission and obligation without conjugating anything, and they're the fastest route to saying real things.

## Why it matters

Lessons 7 and 8 asked a lot of you. This one gives some back: these words don't change form at all. Learn six words and a pattern, and you can suddenly express most of what you want to say in a day.

## The pseudo-verbs

They're called *pseudo-verbs* because they behave like verbs but never take aspect affixes.

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **gusto** | want, like |
| **ayaw** | don't want, dislike |
| **kailangan** | need |
| **puwede** (**pwede**) | can, may, it's possible |
| **dapat** | should, ought to |
| **kaya** | can, be capable of |

### With a noun: use the NG set

> **Gusto ko ng kape.** — "I want coffee."
> **Ayaw niya ng isda.** — "He/She doesn't like fish."
> **Kailangan namin ng tulong.** — "We need help."

### With another verb: use the NG set + linker

The second verb goes in its **basic form** — no aspect:

> **Gusto kong kumain.** — "I want to eat." *(ko + -ng)*
> **Kailangan kong mag-aral.** — "I need to study."
> **Ayaw niyang pumunta.** — "He/She doesn't want to go."
> **Dapat kang matulog.** — "You should sleep."

> ⚠️ **puwede** and **dapat** break the pattern: they take the **ANG** set, not the NG set. **Puwede ba akong umupo?** — "May I sit down?" (not *~~puwede ko~~*). It's a small irregularity, but a frequent one.

### Liking a person

> **Gusto kita.** — "I like you." *(remember **kita** = I → you)*

Worth knowing that this sits somewhere between "I like you" and "I have feelings for you." **Mahal kita** is the unambiguous "I love you," and it's not thrown around lightly.

### Contractions you'll hear constantly

**ayaw ko** almost always becomes **ayoko** in speech:

> **Ayoko.** — "I don't want to." / "No thanks."
> **Ayoko ng maanghang.** — "I don't like spicy food."

And **puwede** is usually said and written **pwede**.

## Asking permission politely

**Pwede ba** + linked verb is the workhorse:

> **Pwede ba akong magtanong?** — "May I ask something?"
> **Pwede po bang humingi ng tubig?** — "May I ask for some water, please?"
> **Pwede na ba?** — "Is this okay now?" / "Can we go?"

That **po** is the politeness particle — lesson 13 covers it properly, but slip it in whenever you're talking to someone older or serving you, and you'll be treated warmly.

## Ability: the ma- and naka- verbs

Some verbs express perception or ability, and they use **ma-** rather than *-um-* or *mag-*. Their completed form starts with **na-**:

| Basic | Completed | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **makita** | **nakita** | to see |
| **marinig** | **narinig** | to hear |
| **maalala** | **naalala** | to remember |
| **matulog** | **natulog** | to sleep |
| **maligo** | **naligo** | to bathe |
| **magutom** | **nagutom** | to get hungry |
| **mapagod** | **napagod** | to get tired |

> **Nakita ko siya kahapon.** — "I saw him/her yesterday."
> **Hindi ko narinig.** — "I didn't hear it."
> **Natulog ako nang maaga.** — "I slept early."
> **Nagutom ako.** — "I got hungry."

Notice **nakita ko** and **narinig ko** take the NG-set pronoun — these are object-focus forms, which is why "I" appears as **ko**. Seeing and hearing spotlight the thing perceived, which is quite logical when you think about it: what matters is *what* you saw.

> 💡 **ma-** also carries a sense of *accidental* or *managed to*, as opposed to deliberate action. **Nabasa ko ang libro** leans toward "I happened to read / got to read the book," where **Binasa ko** is straightforwardly "I read it." This nuance is worth knowing about; it'll sharpen with exposure rather than study.

## Telling someone to do something

**Commands** use the verb's **basic form**, usually with **ka** or **mo**:

> **Kumain ka!** — "Eat!" *(actor focus — eat, generally)*
> **Kainin mo!** — "Eat it!" *(object focus — that specific thing)*
> **Halika!** — "Come here!"
> **Tara!** — "Let's go!" *(extremely common, very casual)*
> **Upo ka.** / **Umupo ka.** — "Sit down."
> **Tulungan mo ako.** — "Help me."

Adding **po** and **naman** softens a command a great deal:

> **Pakitulong naman po.** — "Please help, if you would."

**Paki-** attached to a verb means "please do this" and is one of the most useful prefixes in the language:

> **Pakiabot ng asin.** — "Please pass the salt."
> **Pakisara ang pinto.** — "Please close the door."

**Negative commands** use **huwag** (often **'wag**), not *hindi*:

> **Huwag kang mag-alala.** — "Don't worry."
> **'Wag mo 'yang gawin.** — "Don't do that."
> **Huwag po.** — "Please don't."

> ⚠️ **hindi** negates statements; **huwag** negates commands; **wala** negates having/existing. Three different negatives, three different jobs — mixing them up is one of the clearest learner tells.

## Key takeaways

- **gusto, ayaw, kailangan, puwede, dapat, kaya** never change form.
- With a noun they take the **NG** set (*Gusto ko ng kape*); with a verb, NG set + **linker** + basic verb (*Gusto kong kumain*).
- **puwede** and **dapat** are exceptions — they take the **ANG** set: *Puwede ba akong umupo?*
- **ayaw ko** → **ayoko** in speech; **puwede** → **pwede**.
- **ma-** verbs (perception/ability) take **na-** when completed: *nakita, narinig, natulog*.
- Commands use the **basic form**; **paki-** = "please do"; soften with **po** and **naman**.
- Three negatives, three jobs: **hindi** (statements), **huwag/'wag** (commands), **wala** (having/existing).
