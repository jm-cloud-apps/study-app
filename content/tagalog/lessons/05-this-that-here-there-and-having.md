# This, That, Here, There — and How to Say You Have Something

> **The one-sentence version:** Tagalog splits "that" into two words depending on how far away it is, and handles "there is" and "I have" with the same little word: **may**.

## Why it matters

Pointing at things is how beginners survive. *"How much is that?" "What's this?" "Is there a bathroom?" "Do you have rice?"* — this lesson is the toolkit for all of it, and it's satisfyingly small.

## Three distances, not two

English has **this** and **that**. Tagalog has three, based on distance from the speaker *and* the listener:

| | Meaning | Roughly |
|---|---|---|
| **ito** | this | near me |
| **iyan** | that | near you |
| **iyon** | that (over there) | far from us both |

> **Ano ito?** — "What's this?" *(I'm holding it)*
> **Ano iyan?** — "What's that?" *(you're holding it)*
> **Ano iyon?** — "What's that?" *(it's across the room)*

In speech and texting these get squashed constantly: **'to**, **'yan**, **'yon** (also written **yun**, **'yun**). *"Ano 'to?"* is what you'll actually hear.

The matching place words follow exactly the same three-way split:

| | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **dito** | here (by me) |
| **diyan** | there (by you) |
| **doon** | over there (far from both) |

> **Dito ka!** — "Come here!" / "Stay here!"
> **Nandiyan ba siya?** — "Is he/she there?"
> **Doon tayo kumain.** — "Let's eat over there."

> 💡 Don't over-think the middle one. If **iyan/diyan** feels uncertain, Filipinos won't blink at **ito** or **iyon**. The distinction becomes intuitive from use, not from study.

## Where things are: nasa and nandito

To say something **is located** somewhere, use **nasa** + place:

| Tagalog | English |
|---|---|
| **Nasa bahay ako.** | I'm at home. |
| **Nasa Maynila siya.** | He/She is in Manila. |
| **Nasa mesa ang pagkain.** | The food is on the table. |
| **Nasa trabaho sila.** | They're at work. |

Before a person's name, **nasa** becomes **nasa kay** — but far more common is asking with the location words themselves, which have their own "is here/there" forms:

- **nandito** (also **nandito/narito**) — is here
- **nandiyan** (**nariyan**) — is there
- **nandoon** (**naroon**) — is over there

> **Nandito na ako!** — "I'm here!"
> **Nandoon ang palengke.** — "The market is over there."

## may and mayroon: "there is" and "have"

Here's the efficient bit: Tagalog uses **one** structure for both English "there is/are" and "to have."

**may** + thing:

| Tagalog | English |
|---|---|
| **May tao.** | There's someone. |
| **May bahay ako.** | I have a house. |
| **May kotse siya.** | He/She has a car. |
| **May klase ako bukas.** | I have class tomorrow. |

**mayroon** is the longer form, used when a pronoun comes right after, joined by the linker **-ng**:

> **Mayroon akong bahay.** — "I have a house."

Both sentences above are correct and mean the same thing; **may bahay ako** is shorter and more common in speech. In casual talk **mayroon** often shrinks to **meron**:

> **Meron ka bang pera?** — "Do you have money?"

## Not having: wala

**wala** is the opposite of both **may** and **mayroon** — "there isn't" and "don't have":

| Tagalog | English |
|---|---|
| **Walang tao.** | There's nobody. |
| **Wala akong pera.** | I don't have money. |
| **Wala siyang kotse.** | He/She doesn't have a car. |
| **Wala.** | None. / Nothing. / There isn't any. |

> ⚠️ Don't negate **may** with **hindi**. *~~Hindi ako may pera~~* is wrong. Possession and existence are negated with **wala**, always.

**Wala** on its own is one of the most-used words in the country — the answer to "is there any left?", "what's up?", and "what did you do today?" all at once.

And a phrase you'll hear daily:

> **Walang anuman.** — "You're welcome." *(literally: "it's nothing")*

## Asking where: saan and nasaan

Two question words, and the difference is worth getting right:

- **Saan** — "where," for *actions*. **Saan ka pupunta?** — "Where are you going?"
- **Nasaan** — "where," for *location of a thing*. **Nasaan ang banyo?** — "Where's the bathroom?"

Both get answered with **sa** or a location word:

> **Nasaan ang banyo?** — **Nasa likod.** ("It's at the back.")
> **Saan ka pupunta?** — **Sa palengke.** ("To the market.")

## Ten words to carry around

| Tagalog | English | | Tagalog | English |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **banyo** | bathroom | | **pera** | money |
| **palengke** | wet market | | **tubig** | water |
| **tindahan** | store | | **kanin** | cooked rice |
| **mesa** | table | | **bigas** | uncooked rice |
| **kotse** | car | | **oras** | time, hour |

> 💡 Rice has (at least) three words: **bigas** is uncooked, **kanin** is cooked, **palay** is the growing plant. A language tells you what its speakers pay attention to.

## Putting it together

> **A: Excuse me po, nasaan ang banyo?**
> *Excuse me, where's the bathroom?*
>
> **B: Doon po, sa likod ng tindahan.**
> *Over there, behind the store.*
>
> **A: May tubig ba kayo?**
> *Do you have water?*
>
> **B: Meron po. Magkano? Beinte lang po.**
> *Yes we do. How much? Just twenty.*

You can now handle a real errand.

## Key takeaways

- Three distances: **ito** (this, by me), **iyan** (that, by you), **iyon** (that, far) — and place words **dito / diyan / doon**.
- **nasa** + place = "is located at"; **nandito / nandiyan / nandoon** = "is here / there / over there."
- **may** and **mayroon** cover both "there is" and "have." **Meron** is the casual form of *mayroon*.
- Negate them with **wala**, never with *hindi*. **Wala akong pera.**
- **Walang anuman** = "you're welcome."
- **Saan** asks where an action happens; **Nasaan** asks where a thing is.
