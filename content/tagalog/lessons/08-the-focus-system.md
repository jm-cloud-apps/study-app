# Verbs, Part 2: The Focus System (the Heart of Tagalog)

> **The one-sentence version:** Tagalog lets you choose which participant in an event gets the spotlight — the doer, the thing, the place, the beneficiary — and the verb changes its affix to announce your choice.

## Why it matters

This is the concept that makes Tagalog *Tagalog*. It's also the reason a learner who knows plenty of vocabulary can still sound distinctly foreign: they put the doer in the spotlight every time, because that's what English does. Getting comfortable here is the difference between speaking Tagalog and speaking English with Tagalog words.

Take it slowly. Nobody absorbs this in one sitting, and that's expected.

## The same event, two camera angles

Consider one event: *I ate the mango.*

> **Kumain ako ng mangga.**
> *ate / I(spotlight) / a mango* — "I ate a mango."

> **Kinain ko ang mangga.**
> *ate / by-me / the mango(spotlight)* — "I ate the mango."

Same event, same participants. What changed:

| | Verb | Who has **ang** | Who has **ng** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sentence 1 | **kumain** (*-um-*) | **ako** — me | **ng mangga** — the mango |
| Sentence 2 | **kinain** (*-in*) | **ang mangga** — the mango | **ko** — me |

🔑 **The affix on the verb tells you which participant is wearing `ang`.** That's the entire mechanism. The verb and the marker agree; they're two halves of the same signal.

This is why the pronoun sets from lesson 3 exist. When *I* am the spotlight, I'm **ako** (ANG set). When I'm demoted to background doer, I'm **ko** (NG set). The pronoun changes because the role changed.

## The four focuses you'll actually use

| Focus | Spotlight is… | Typical affix | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Actor** | the doer | **-um-**, **mag-** | **Bumili ako ng libro.** — I bought a book. |
| **Object** | the thing acted on | **-in** | **Binili ko ang libro.** — I bought the book. |
| **Locative** | the place or target | **-an** | **Pinuntahan ko ang tindahan.** — I went to the store. |
| **Benefactive** | who it's for, or the thing moved | **i-** | **Ibinigay ko ang libro.** — I gave the book. |

Actor and object focus carry most of everyday speech. Learn those two properly and you can function; the other two will settle in with exposure.

## Building object-focus (-in) verbs

The aspects work like everything else — an affix plus reduplication. The completed form uses the infix **-in-** placed after the first consonant:

| Root | Basic | **Completed** | **Progressive** | **Contemplated** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **kain** (eat) | **kainin** | **kinain** | **kinakain** | **kakainin** |
| **basa** (read) | **basahin** | **binasa** | **binabasa** | **babasahin** |
| **gawa** (do/make) | **gawin** | **ginawa** | **ginagawa** | **gagawin** |
| **luto** (cook) | **lutuin** | **niluto** | **niluluto** | **lulutuin** |

> 💡 Notice **luto** → **niluto**, not *~~linuto~~*. When a root starts with **l** (also **r**, **w**, **y**, and often **n**), the infix *-in-* becomes a **ni-** prefix instead. It's a pronunciation accommodation, and there are only a handful of these roots in daily use.

You'll also see the basic form take **-in**, **-hin**, or **-nin** depending on the root's ending (*kainin*, *basahin*, *gawin*) — that's just sound-smoothing, learned per word.

## How do you choose?

Here's the rule of thumb that gets beginners 80% of the way:

> 🔑 **If the thing being acted on is specific and known — "the" rather than "a" — Filipinos usually reach for object focus.**

> **Bumili ako ng tinapay.** — "I bought some bread." *(unspecific)*
> **Binili ko ang tinapay.** — "I bought the bread." *(the one we both know about)*

> **Kumain ka ng gulay!** — "Eat some vegetables!"
> **Kainin mo ang gulay!** — "Eat the vegetables!" *(the ones on your plate)*

That second pair is instructive: **Kainin mo ang gulay** is what a Filipino mother actually says. A learner who only knows actor focus would say *Kumain ka ng gulay*, which is grammatical, but points at vegetables in general rather than the ones going cold in front of you.

Because English almost always keeps the doer as subject, learners under-use object focus dramatically. If you catch yourself starting every sentence with an actor-focus verb, that's the habit to break.

## Recognizing focus in the wild

You'll meet these constantly. Practice reading the marker, not the word order:

| Tagalog | Spotlight | English |
|---|---|---|
| **Nagluto siya ng adobo.** | siya | She cooked adobo. |
| **Niluto niya ang adobo.** | ang adobo | She cooked the adobo. |
| **Tinawagan ko si Nanay.** | si Nanay | I called Mom. |
| **Binigyan ko siya ng pera.** | siya | I gave him/her money. |
| **Ibinigay ko sa kaniya ang pera.** | ang pera | I gave the money to him/her. |

Look at the last two: **binigyan** (*-an*) spotlights the *recipient*; **ibinigay** (*i-*) spotlights the *thing given*. Same event, different camera.

## A gentle reality check

You will not get this right consistently for a while, and that is completely normal — this is the part of Tagalog that takes the longest for everyone, including Filipinos raised abroad. Two things that genuinely help:

1. **Learn verbs in pairs**, actor and object focus together: *kumain / kinain*, *bumili / binili*, *nagluto / niluto*. It doubles your memorization but halves your confusion.
2. **Listen for `ang`.** When you hear a sentence, find the **ang** phrase and ask "is that the doer or the thing?" Then look back at the verb's affix. Fifty rounds of that and the system clicks.

You will be understood long before you're accurate. Filipinos are notably generous with learners — genuinely delighted, in most cases, that you're trying at all.

## Check yourself

1. In **Binili ko ang libro**, who bought the book, and which word tells you?
2. Your friend's plate has fish on it and you want to say "Eat the fish." Which focus should you use, and why does the actor-focus version feel wrong?
3. Why does **ako** become **ko** when the sentence switches to object focus?

## Key takeaways

- Tagalog verbs mark **which participant is in the spotlight** — the one wearing **ang**.
- Actor focus (**-um-**, **mag-**) spotlights the doer; object focus (**-in**) spotlights the thing; **-an** spotlights place/recipient; **i-** spotlights the thing given or the beneficiary.
- The verb affix and the **ang** marker always agree — two halves of one signal.
- Pronouns switch sets accordingly: spotlight = **ako**, background doer = **ko**.
- Rule of thumb: **definite object → object focus.** *Kainin mo ang gulay*, not *Kumain ka ng gulay*.
- Object-focus completed forms use the infix **-in-** (*kinain*), which becomes **ni-** before roots starting with *l, r, w, y*: **niluto**.
- Learn verbs in actor/object **pairs**, and listen for where **ang** lands.
