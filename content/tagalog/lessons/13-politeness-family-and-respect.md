# Politeness, Family, and Respect

> **The one-sentence version:** Filipino politeness runs on one small word — **po** — plus a habit of addressing almost everyone by a family term, and getting these right matters more socially than getting your grammar right.

## Why it matters

You can make plenty of grammatical mistakes and be received warmly. Skip **po** with an elder and something subtler happens — not offense exactly, but a small note that you weren't raised with it. This lesson is the cheapest possible investment in being liked.

## po and opo

**po** is not a word with a meaning. It's a marker of respect that you sprinkle into a sentence when speaking to someone older, someone in authority, a customer, or a stranger you want to be gracious to.

> **Salamat po.** — Thank you (respectfully).
> **Oo** → **Opo.** — Yes (respectfully).
> **Hindi po.** — No (respectfully).
> **Kumusta po kayo?** — How are you? (respectfully)
> **Pasensya na po.** — I'm sorry / please excuse me.

It slots in with the other particles from lesson 10, usually after the first word or phrase:

> **Hindi pa po ako kumakain.** — "I haven't eaten yet."
> **Pwede po bang magtanong?** — "May I ask something?"

**ho** is a softer, slightly less formal variant of **po** — used with people you respect but are comfortable with. If you're unsure, **po** is never wrong.

> 🔑 **When in doubt, use po.** Over-politeness costs nothing in the Philippines. Under-politeness with an elder is one of the few genuine social stumbles available to a foreigner.

Notice also **kayo** in *"Kumusta po kayo?"* — the plural "you," used for a single person as a mark of respect, the way French uses *vous*. With an older person, plural forms (**kayo, ninyo, inyo**) are the warmer choice.

## Everyone is family

Here's the thing that surprises learners most: you address people by kinship terms even when they're not related to you.

| Term | Literally | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Kuya** | older brother | any slightly older man — the waiter, the driver, your friend's brother |
| **Ate** (*AH-teh*) | older sister | any slightly older woman |
| **Tito / Tita** | uncle / aunt | your parents' friends, close family friends |
| **Lolo / Lola** | grandfather / grandmother | any elderly person |
| **Nanay / Tatay** | mother / father | parents (also **Inay/Itay**, **Mama/Papa**, **Mommy/Daddy**) |
| **Manong / Manang** | (older man/woman) | older strangers, esp. vendors, drivers |
| **Anak** | child | what a parent calls their child, any age |

> **Kuya, magkano po?** — "Excuse me [older brother], how much?"
> **Salamat po, Ate.** — "Thank you, ma'am."

Calling a stranger **Kuya** or **Ate** is friendly and normal — it's how you say "excuse me" to a person whose name you don't know. Not using a term at all can feel oddly cold.

> ⚠️ Small trap: calling someone **Tita** or **Lola** who doesn't consider themselves that old is a mild insult in the same way "ma'am" can be in English. When ages are close, **Ate** and **Kuya** are the safe choices.

Two more terms with real emotional weight: **panganay** (eldest child) and **bunso** (youngest). Filipinos will tell you which they are, and it explains a surprising amount.

## Mano po

**Mano** (or **pagmamano**) is a gesture of respect to an elder: you take the back of their hand and touch it lightly to your forehead, saying **"Mano po."**

Younger generations often say **"Bless po"** or **"Bless, Tita"** for the same gesture. You'll see it at family gatherings, on arriving and leaving. If an elder offers their hand, this is what's happening.

## Greetings and goodbyes

| Tagalog | English |
|---|---|
| **Magandang umaga po.** | Good morning. |
| **Magandang tanghali po.** | Good noon. *(a real greeting here)* |
| **Magandang hapon po.** | Good afternoon. |
| **Magandang gabi po.** | Good evening. |
| **Kumusta po kayo?** | How are you? |
| **Mabuti naman po.** | I'm well, thank you. |
| **Ingat.** | Take care. *(the everyday goodbye)* |
| **Ingat ka sa daan.** | Take care on the road. |
| **Paalam.** | Farewell. *(formal, weighty — not casual)* |
| **Sige, una na ako.** | Okay, I'll head off first. |
| **Balik ka ha?** | Come back, okay? |

> 💡 **Paalam** is not the everyday goodbye — it carries the weight of a real parting. The normal ways to leave are **"Sige"**, **"Ingat"**, or **"Una na ako"** ("I'll go ahead"). Announcing your departure rather than slipping out is itself a politeness.

## Sorry, thanks, and excuse me

| Tagalog | Use |
|---|---|
| **Salamat.** / **Salamat po.** | Thank you. |
| **Maraming salamat po.** | Thank you very much. |
| **Walang anuman.** | You're welcome. |
| **Pasensya na po.** | Sorry / please bear with me. *(softer, very common)* |
| **Patawad.** | Forgive me. *(heavy — for real wrongs)* |
| **Excuse me po** / **Makikiraan po.** | Excuse me (passing through). |

**Makikiraan po** — "I'll just pass through" — is what you say squeezing past someone in a crowded jeepney or corridor. Wonderfully specific, and immediately useful.

> 💡 In practice, English "sorry" and "thank you" are used constantly in Manila, with **po** attached: *"Sorry po," "Thank you po."* That's completely natural speech, not a failure to learn the Tagalog.

## The invitation you must know

When Filipinos are eating and you walk in, they will say:

> **Kain tayo!** — "Let's eat!"
> **Tara, kain!** — "Come on, eat!"

This is offered to everyone, always, whether or not there's enough food. The gracious replies:

> **Sige, salamat po!** — accepting.
> **Salamat po, busog pa ako.** — "Thanks, I'm still full." *(declining warmly)*

Declining is fine. Ignoring the offer is not — it's an invitation to be part of things, and a brief reply acknowledges it.

## Three ideas worth knowing about

These are widely discussed Filipino social concepts. They're not rules you follow so much as currents you'll feel:

- **Hiya** — a sense of propriety and social shame; not wanting to impose or be embarrassed, or to embarrass someone else. It's why requests and refusals are often indirect.
- **Pakikisama** — getting along, going with the group, maintaining smooth relations.
- **Utang na loob** — a "debt of the inside": deep, lasting gratitude for a significant kindness, felt as an ongoing bond rather than a transaction to settle.

Understanding these explains a lot of the indirectness in Filipino conversation — the softened refusals, the **baka** ("maybe") that means no, the **"sige, sige"** that's keeping the peace. Being gentle, indirect, and generous with **po** will serve you well.

> 💡 One practical consequence: a direct "no" is rare. **"Baka mamaya"** ("maybe later"), **"tingnan natin"** ("let's see"), or a hesitant **"medyo mahirap"** ("it's a bit difficult") are often polite refusals. Listen for hesitation rather than for the word *hindi*.

## Check yourself

1. You're buying fruit from a woman about your mother's age. What do you call her, and what particle should be in your sentence?
2. What's the difference in weight between **Pasensya na** and **Patawad**?
3. Your friend's family is eating when you arrive and they call **"Kain tayo!"** — what are two acceptable replies?

## Key takeaways

- **po** marks respect and has no meaning of its own; **opo** = respectful yes. **ho** is its softer cousin. When in doubt, use **po**.
- Plural **kayo/ninyo/inyo** used for one person is a mark of respect.
- Address strangers with kinship terms: **Kuya, Ate, Tito, Tita, Lolo, Lola, Manong, Manang**.
- **Mano po** (or "Bless po") is the hand-to-forehead gesture of respect to elders.
- **Ingat** is the everyday goodbye; **Paalam** is heavy and formal.
- **Pasensya na po** for everyday apologies; **Patawad** for serious ones. **Makikiraan po** to pass by.
- **Kain tayo!** is always offered — always answer it, accepting or declining warmly.
- **Hiya**, **pakikisama**, and **utang na loob** explain much of the indirectness in conversation; a soft "maybe" is often a no.
