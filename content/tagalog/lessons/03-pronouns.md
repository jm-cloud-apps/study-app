# Pronouns: Three Sets, and the Famous "We"

> **The one-sentence version:** Tagalog pronouns come in three parallel sets — one for the subject, one for the doer/owner, one for direction — and Tagalog makes a distinction English can't: two different words for "we."

## Why it matters

Pronouns are the highest-mileage words in any language; you'll say **ako**, **ka**, and **niya** thousands of times. And because Tagalog has three sets rather than one, learning them as a *table* now saves you from re-learning them piecemeal for months.

Don't try to memorize all 21 forms today. Learn the first column, get comfortable, and let the other two arrive as they become useful.

## The three sets

| | **ANG set** (subject) | **NG set** (doer / possessor) | **SA set** (to / for / direction) |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | **ako** | **ko** | **akin** |
| you (sing.) | **ikaw / ka** | **mo** | **iyo** |
| he / she | **siya** | **niya** | **kaniya** |
| we (not you) | **kami** | **namin** | **amin** |
| we (incl. you) | **tayo** | **natin** | **atin** |
| you (plural) | **kayo** | **ninyo** | **inyo** |
| they | **sila** | **nila** | **kanila** |

Roughly, and enough to work with for now:

- **ANG set** — the one the sentence is *about*. *"**Ako** si Miguel."*
- **NG set** — who *did* it, or who *owns* it. *"bahay **ko**" — my house.*
- **SA set** — where it's going, who it's for. *"para sa **iyo**" — for you.*

Those roles will get much sharper in the next lesson, which is about the markers **ang**, **ng**, and **sa** themselves. The pronouns and the markers are the same system.

## The one that matters most: kami vs tayo

English has one "we" and it's permanently ambiguous. Tagalog has two, and Filipinos hear the difference instantly.

- **tayo** = we, **including you**, the person I'm talking to.
- **kami** = we, **excluding you**.

> **Kakain na tayo!** — "Let's eat!" *(you're coming)*
> **Kakain na kami.** — "We're going to eat." *(you're not included — we're leaving you)*

🔑 This is the single most emotionally loaded grammar point in the language. Using **kami** where **tayo** belongs can land as a small exclusion — "we're going, not you." Using **tayo** is warm and inclusive. When in doubt with people you like, **tayo** is the kinder error.

The same split runs through the other sets: **namin/atin**, **natin/amin**. Same rule every time — the **t-** forms (*tayo, natin, atin*) include the listener.

## ikaw vs ka — position, not politeness

Both mean "you," and they are **not** more or less polite than each other. The rule is purely about where in the sentence the word sits:

- **ikaw** when it comes **first**.
- **ka** everywhere else.

> **Ikaw ang guro.** — "*You're* the teacher."
> **Guro ka.** — "You're a teacher."
> **Kumusta ka?** — "How are you?"

You'd never say *~~Guro ikaw~~* or *~~Ikaw ang guro ka~~*. Once you notice the pattern it becomes automatic.

> 💡 There's no formal/informal "you" as in Spanish *tú/usted* or French *tu/vous*. Politeness in Tagalog is carried by the particle **po** instead — lesson 13. So **ka** is fine for your grandmother, as long as there's a **po** in the sentence: *"Kumusta po kayo?"* (Though for elders, many families also use plural **kayo** as a respectful singular — the same instinct as French *vous*.)

## Possession: the NG set, placed after

To say "my house," put the NG-set pronoun **after** the noun:

| Tagalog | English |
|---|---|
| **bahay ko** | my house |
| **pangalan mo** | your name |
| **kaibigan niya** | his/her friend |
| **anak namin** | our child (not yours) |
| **bahay nila** | their house |

So **Ano'ng pangalan mo?** is literally *"What the name your?"* — and now that sentence from lesson 2 has fully decoded itself.

For emphasis or contrast, the SA set can carry possession too, placed in front: **akin ang bahay** ("the house is *mine*"). You'll meet this most often in the fixed phrase **sa akin** — "mine / to me."

## Putting them to work

| Tagalog | English |
|---|---|
| **Mabait siya.** | He/She is kind. |
| **Estudyante kami.** | We're students. *(not you)* |
| **Guro sila.** | They're teachers. |
| **Maganda ang bahay ninyo.** | Your (pl.) house is beautiful. |
| **Kaibigan kita.** | I'm your friend. |

That last one hides a treat. **kita** is a special single word meaning **"I → you"** — it replaces what would otherwise be an awkward *ko + ikaw*. It's the word in the sentence every learner wants first:

> **Mahal kita.** — "I love you."

Literally *"beloved I-you."* One word doing the work of two pronouns, and now you know exactly why it's built that way.

## Check yourself

1. Your friend is standing next to you and you want to say "Let's go." Do you use **tayo** or **kami**? What would the other one imply?
2. Fix this: *"Mabait ikaw."*
3. How would you say "her house" and "our (including you) teacher"?

## Key takeaways

- Pronouns come in three parallel sets: **ANG** (subject), **NG** (doer/possessor), **SA** (direction/benefit).
- **tayo** = we *including* you; **kami** = we *excluding* you. The **t-** forms include the listener.
- **ikaw** goes first in the sentence, **ka** goes elsewhere — position, not politeness.
- There's no formal "you"; politeness comes from **po**, and sometimes from using plural **kayo** for an elder.
- Possession = noun + NG-set pronoun: **bahay ko** (my house), **pangalan mo** (your name).
- **kita** is a special "I → you" pronoun: **Mahal kita.**
